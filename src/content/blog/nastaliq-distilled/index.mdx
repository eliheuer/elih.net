A previous post introduced the [NeuralType](/blog/neuraltype)
concept: a font can be a small neural network instead of a table of
outlines. The first .ntf font used [square Kufic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufic#Square_Kufic), a style that lives
on a binary grid and resembles low-resolution pixel art. On its own, that is easy to write off as a neat
trick, not a serious challenge to the OpenType format. This post is
the next step. It makes the case that a neural font can do the work
of a real OpenType font. We convert a standard OpenType font into a .ntf neural network based font, in
the style that OpenType serves worst: nastaliq. The target
is [Gulzar](https://github.com/googlefonts/Gulzar), an OFL licensed nastaliq
by Borna Izadpanah, Fiona Ross, Alice Savoie, and Simon Cozens.

<NeuralTypeDemo {...nastaliqDemo} />

Nastaliq is the correct target because it is one of the styles that
OpenType limits the most. It is a cursive, flowing style: letters
join or break by the rules of the Arabic script, and a word is a
chain of connected runs. Within a run, each letter hangs below the letter before it, so a
long run starts high above the line and steps down until its last
letter lands on it. In its handwritten form a baseline grid barely exists. The line a
word sits on can be straight or curved, and letterforms, dots, and
swashes move freely around it to fill the space in a pleasing way.
OpenType approximates this behavior with more than
one thousand glyphs and cursive-attachment rules. Even so, no
OpenType nastaliq font has come anywhere near the style as it
developed in manuscript and calligraphy culture. If a neural font
has value anywhere, it has value here.

The demo above runs the full pipeline in your browser. The font is
a model with 1.36 million parameters, stored as a 5.4 MB .ntf file.
The engine is Rust compiled to WASM. It runs the model, places each
letter in the cascade, and traces the contours. The result is real text: click
the canvas, place the cursor, select, copy, and paste. One caveat: the
model has only been trained on a small set of words so far: every
word up to three letters long, plus some common longer ones. Words
it has not been trained on render rough, because parts of a long
word sit in contexts the short words never showed the model, and
there it has to guess. This is a training-data problem, not a limit
of the format, and the fix is to grow the training set toward real
Urdu and Persian text.

NeuralType is a general format, and nastaliq is one use case among
many. I am using it for the early demo fonts because it makes the strongest case. The goal is
not to reproduce existing OpenType nastaliq fonts. The goal is fonts
that do justice to the manuscript and calligraphy tradition that nastaliq comes
from, and no OpenType font does.

Distillation of an existing OFL nastaliq font is a technical
exercise. If the distilled font matches its teacher, the format
works. After that, the next step is new nastaliq fonts, designed to
show what a NeuralType font can do that an OpenType font cannot. Distillation also answers
the file-size question early: how many parameters does nastaliq
need?

### The Distillation Process: Measuring Gulzar

The first step toward a nastaliq .ntf font is to measure the
problem: how much contextual behavior does Gulzar contain? To answer
this, we shaped every two-letter word through Gulzar
with [harfrust](https://github.com/harfbuzz/harfrust) and counted
how many different glyphs each letter takes at the start of the
word. The count depends on the letter that follows: ن is drawn with
16 different glyphs, ي with 15, م with 14. Across all 31 letters,
Gulzar uses 344 distinct word-initial glyphs. Amiri, a naskh with
rich contextual behavior, uses 150 on the same test.

The figure below shows word-initial ن in green, with the following
letter in gray. The follower selects the shape.

![Fourteen ordered pairs. The green word-initial ن takes a different shape for each gray follower.](./noon-variants.png)

Counting glyphs measures one half of the problem: how many shapes
there are. The other half is how far words climb. The shaped word
نستعليق places glyphs at vertical offsets up to **1.38 em above the
baseline**. One word spans more than a full em of vertical travel.
That geometry strains the metal-type box that digital font formats
still emulate. Numbers like this are why we are trying a new format.

The figure below shows the shaped word نستعليق. The letters
alternate green and gray, the red chain joins the letter origins, and
the gray rule is the baseline.

![The word nastaliq shaped by Gulzar: letters step down from high above the baseline, and the final swash lands below it.](./cascade.png)

### Extraction

Distillation needs a teacher: a working system whose output the new
model learns to copy. Here the teacher is not the Gulzar font file
by itself. It is Gulzar plus HarfBuzz, the shaping engine that reads
text, consults the rule tables in the font, and answers: these
glyphs, at these positions. The same text always shapes to the same
result, and shaping a word takes a fraction of a millisecond, so
training data can be generated in any quantity.

The extraction tool (`crates/neuraltype-distill`) shapes every
short word. The corpus is every single letter, every two-letter
word, and every three-letter word: 30,783 small words. The
three-letter words matter because they are the shortest text where
a letter has a neighbor on each side. Those neighbors produce the medial forms. A second pass added
24 real words: the bismillah, Al-Fatiha, Al-Ikhlas, and نستعليق. The
full corpus is 30,807 words.

For every letter in every word, the tool records two facts. The first
fact is the composed shape: the base letterform and its dots, as one
unit. The second fact is where the letter sits relative to the letter
before it: how far across, and how far down. Following those offsets
letter by letter rebuilds the downward cascade of the word.

The dataset holds 91,390 letter-in-context records, 416 unique
glyphs, and **1,150 unique composed shapes**. More than 100 of those
shapes belong to خ alone. Between adjacent letters, the cascade drops
as much as 1.66 em.

The figure below shows every composed shape of خ in the dataset, 108
in total.

![All 108 composed shapes of خ in the dataset, in a grid.](./kha-shapes.png)

One measurement set the size of the model's input. Given a letter
and two neighbors on each side, Gulzar always draws exactly one
shape, so a five-letter window is all the context there is to learn
from this teacher. That number measures Gulzar, not the goal. The
goal is calligraphy-quality text on the web, and a calligrapher is
aware of everything: the whole word, the line, the space on the
page. A neural font can grow toward that by taking more of the text
as input. An OpenType font cannot grow that way, no matter how many rules
are added, because it is built on the metal-type model of fixed
shapes in fixed rectangles, and that model was never the right one
for this tradition.

### Shapes as Fields

Extraction leaves us with 1,150 shapes, stored as bézier outlines.
The model has to learn to output these shapes, and that raises the
central design question of the project: output them in what form?
An outline is a list of curve points, and the list is a different
length for every shape. That is a hard thing for a small neural
network to produce: get one point wrong and the whole contour
breaks. A neural network learns best when the output is a fixed
grid of numbers, where a small change in the weights makes a small
change in the picture. So we do not ask the model to write curves.
We ask it to paint a picture that curves can be recovered from
exactly.

We render each of the 1,150 shapes to a signed-distance field. It
looks like a soft grayscale image of the letter, but the gray means
something different. In an ordinary image, each pixel says how much
ink is at that spot, and pixels away from the letter's edge say
nothing useful. In a signed-distance field, each cell holds the
distance to the nearest edge of the shape: positive inside, negative
outside. Every cell knows where the edge is, even cells far from it.
The outline of the letter is the line where the field crosses zero,
and because the field changes smoothly, that line can be found
between cells, at finer than pixel precision. A small error in the
model's output nudges the edge slightly instead of punching a hole
in the letter. This field is the continuous version of the binary
grid in the first NeuralType font: figure and ground, with no stroke
or skeleton assumptions. This choice is not obvious, and later in
this post an experiment tries the other route, a model that writes
curve points directly.

The image below shows the isolated خ from the training set. On the
left is the shape. On the right is its signed-distance field, with
the zero contour in red. The model learns to paint the picture on
the right.

![The isolated خ from Gulzar, and the same shape as a signed-distance field with the zero contour marked in red.](./sdf-kha.png)

The figure below zooms all the way in. The red box on the left
marks a small patch of the field, and the right side shows that
patch cell by cell. Each cell is one value of the field, printed as
a distance in pixels: negative outside the letter, positive inside.
The red line is the outline, drawn where the values cross zero.
Reading neighboring cells places that crossing at a fraction of a
pixel, which is how a coarse grid still yields a precise edge. This
patch happens to cross a thin part of the stroke, so two edges pass
through it, with the inside of the stroke between them.

![A patch of the distance field enlarged to a grid of numbered cells. Values rise from negative to positive and back, and the red zero contour passes between the cells at interpolated positions.](./sdf-cells.png)

We rendered test shapes at two resolutions and looked. At 64 pixels
per em, the tapers and hairlines of Gulzar survive the threshold. At
96 pixels per em, the result looks the same at reading size, but the
model must output 2.3 times more pixels. So the resolution is 64. The
shared canvas is 155×219 pixels. The canvas is large because nastaliq
is large: swash tails and dots extend far from the origin of each
letter.

Below is the isolated خ at both resolutions, shown at the same size:
64 pixels per em on the left, 96 on the right. The threshold keeps
the tapers at both resolutions.

![The isolated خ thresholded at 64 and 96 pixels per em, at the same size. The two are nearly identical.](./res-64-96.png)

### Training the Student

The model is small, and it works in three steps. First, each of the
five context letters comes in as a learned code, a short list of
numbers the model chooses for each letter during training. Second, a
small network mixes the five codes into one description of the shape
to draw: 256 numbers. Third, a stack of five upscaling layers turns
that description into the field, doubling the size of the picture at
each step. A separate small output predicts the offset to the next
letter. The total is 1.36 million parameters: 5.4 MB stored as
32-bit numbers, against the 963 KB TTF of Gulzar.

The model card:

| nastaliq-gulzar .ntf, v0 (working name) |   |
| --- | --- |
| architecture | 5 context embeddings (24 dims) → MLP (120 → 256) → 5 transposed convolutions (128 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 1 channels, 4×4 kernels, stride 2) + a 2-value displacement head |
| parameters | 1,357,995 |
| file | 5,433,100 bytes: f32 weights + a 1.1 KB JSON header |
| output | one 155×219 signed-distance field at 64 px/em, plus one displacement vector |
| vocabulary | 34 tokens: 31 letters, the لا and لله ligatures, and a boundary token |
| corpus | 30,807 words from the teacher: 91,390 letter-in-context records, 1,150 composed shapes |
| teacher | Gulzar Regular shaped by HarfBuzz ([harfrust](https://github.com/harfbuzz/harfrust)) |
| optimizer | AdamW, lr 3e-4, then a 1e-4 decay pass; batch 128; every 20th record held out |
| compute | one Apple M4 Mac laptop, CPU ([candle](https://github.com/huggingface/candle) accelerate backend), about 8 minutes per epoch. Later runs moved to a Linux PC with an RTX 2060 SUPER, at 100 seconds per epoch |

Training runs in [candle](https://github.com/huggingface/candle), so
the same code trains on CPU, Metal, or CUDA. Inference, running the trained model to generate a
letterform, is plain Rust in the engine, with no machine-learning
framework. A .ntf file is only weights, and running weights is only
arithmetic.

Progress is measured with IoU, intersection over union, the
standard score for shape agreement: the area where the model's ink
and the teacher's ink overlap, divided by the area covered by
either. Identical shapes score 1.0, and shapes with no overlap
score 0.

The first night produced one surprise: the Metal backend of candle
trained far worse than plain CPU. Same code, same data, and at equal
epochs the CPU run had reached an IoU of 0.21 while Metal sat at
0.01. The overnight run moved to CPU, and words were legible by
epoch 12.

Thin strokes form last. The training loss explains why: a hairline
is worth tens of pixels and a bowl is worth thousands, so the model
gets the bowls right first.

The hardest word was الله. Gulzar draws it with a dedicated
ligature, and no word in the first corpus was long enough to trigger
it. Adding the bismillah and other real words put the ligature in
the data, but a few dozen words in a corpus of thirty thousand learn
slowly. The fix was to repeat those words dozens of times per epoch,
and the ligature converged in eight epochs.

Training finished at an IoU of 0.945. A final pass with a decaying learning rate settled the last details,
the same schedule that took the Kufic font from almost correct to
exact. The bismillah now renders better, ligatures included.

The sheet below compares the teacher with the final model. Gulzar is
gray on top. The model is green below.

![The bismillah words, نور, and نستعليق rendered by Gulzar in gray above, and by the final model in green below. The rows match, including the الله ligature.](./teacher-model.png)

### Tracing with img2bez

The model outputs a field, but text needs outlines. The demo uses
two tracers. While you type, a fast sub-pixel tracer draws each word
with no added latency. When input settles, each word upgrades to a
quality outline from [img2bez](/blog/img2bez), the autotracer I
built for turning rasters into font-grade bézier curves. The upgrade
runs in a Web Worker, so the editor never blocks.

The .ntf pipeline pushed one new capability back into img2bez. Its
sub-pixel stage already treated an image as a signed field, so we
removed the image: `trace_sdf` traces the model's distance field
directly. It samples the field lazily, takes exact iso-crossings,
fits the contour faithfully with the type-design cleanup turned off,
and re-splines the result as a periodic cubic spline: every point is
smooth and the curvature is continuous (G2). No raster exists anywhere in the render
path: the model paints a field, and the field becomes curves.

### Three ways to make it bigger, none of which worked

The model in the demo is 1.36 million parameters and reaches a
contour IoU of 0.945. The obvious next move is to make it bigger. We
tried three versions of bigger, and all three came out worse. The
results are worth writing down, because the reasons differ.

A note on why three failures fit in two days. The training moved off
the laptop and onto a Linux PC with a used gaming GPU, which took a
19-minute epoch down to 100 seconds. That is the difference between
testing one idea a day and testing three overnight, and most of what
follows is negative results, which is exactly the kind of work you only
do when a run is cheap.

**More pixels.** We retrained at 96 px/em instead of 64, on the same
architecture. The model grew to 3.10 million parameters and the
letters looked the same. Almost all of the new parameters went to a
single place. The field is painted by a stack of transposed
convolutions that starts from a small seed grid, and that seed comes
from one linear layer. A bigger canvas means a bigger seed, and that
one layer holds 93% of the model. The part that describes a letter,
the 256 numbers the perceptron produces, never changed. The model
knew exactly as much as before and had more pixels to spread it
over.

The deeper reason is that pixel count was never the limit. The model
outputs a distance field, not a bitmap, and the tracer finds the
zero crossing by interpolating between samples. A field at 64 px/em
already locates an edge to a fraction of a pixel. Raising the
resolution improves a number that was not the bottleneck.

**Wider layers.** So we spent the parameters on the description
instead: 512 numbers instead of 256, and double the channels in
every convolution, on the original 64 px/em canvas. That model is
5.36 million parameters, and after 200 epochs it reached 0.809
against the small model's 0.945. It draws heavier strokes and merges
letters that should stay apart. In نستعليق the joints dissolve.

It was not too big for the data. A model that has memorized its
training set shows falling training error alongside rising held-out
error, and both fell together the whole way. It was undertrained and
badly tuned: the same learning rate as a model a quarter its size,
no normalization between the wider layers, and a curve still
climbing when the run ended. At epoch 100 the small model had
reached 0.914 and a held-out error of 0.00011. The wide model needed
200 epochs to reach 0.806 at 0.00032, three times the error.

**A different output entirely.** The third attempt gave up on fields
and predicted outlines directly, in the same spirit as the
[Virtua Grotesk](/blog/virtua-grotesk) model. The teacher's own
béziers become a token stream of drawing commands and quantized
coordinate deltas: 135 tokens of vocabulary, 91,339 sequences, 17
million tokens. A 12.7 million parameter decoder-only transformer
learns to continue that stream from the five-letter context.

It does not draw letters. Next-token accuracy stalled at 0.482 and
two learning rate drops moved it by two thousandths. What comes out
is a scatter of small closed contours. The dots land near where dots
belong, and on longer words you can see fragments following the
downward slope of the nastaliq baseline, so the model has learned
something about where ink goes. It has not learned to close a
stroke.

![Three words. The teacher's outlines on the left, the field model in the middle drawing them almost identically, and the vector model on the right producing only small scattered fragments.](./vector-fragments.png)

| | parameters | file | result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| field, 64 px/em | 1,357,995 | 5.4 MB | IoU 0.945, ships in the demo |
| field, 96 px/em | 3,101,483 | 12.4 MB | IoU 0.825, no visible gain |
| field, 64 px/em, wide | 5,357,091 | 21.4 MB | IoU 0.809, strokes merge |
| vector, outline tokens | 12,693,639 | 53.3 MB | accuracy 0.482, fragments |

The honest summary is that the smallest model is still the best one,
and that none of these runs found the real ceiling. The next suspect
is not the model but what it is taught. Every letter-in-context is
supervised against one shape, chosen as the most common when the
teacher draws more than one. Where Gulzar genuinely varies, the
model is being asked to learn an average of two different letters.
That is a data question, and it comes before any more parameters.
